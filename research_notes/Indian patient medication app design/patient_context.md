# Real-world context of medication adherence among patients in India (as of 2026)

> Scope note: all findings below are India-specific unless explicitly flagged as an international comparison baseline. Where a foundational study predates 2020 it is labelled **[pre-2020 foundational]**. Several figures were obtained from search-engine extracts of paywalled or CAPTCHA-protected journal pages rather than from the full text; these are flagged **[snippet-level, verify in full text]**.

---

## Q1. Scale and demographic profile of chronic disease in India; how many people take 1, 2, 3+ daily medicines

### Takeaway
India has roughly 101 million people with diabetes, 136 million with prediabetes and 315 million with hypertension (ICMR-INDIAB, 2023) — but the number actually *on* daily medicines is far smaller than prevalence, because the treatment cascade leaks badly: in NFHS-5 only ~13% of adults aged 15–49 with hypertension were on treatment. There is no national dataset on how many Indians take 1 vs 2 vs 3+ daily medicines; the only robust pooled figure is polypharmacy (5+ medicines) in 49% of older adults.

### Cited Findings

**Prevalence**
- ICMR-INDIAB (phase-wise national survey, 113,043 adults aged 20+, 31 states/UTs, fieldwork 2008–2020, published *Lancet Diabetes & Endocrinology* June 2023): weighted diabetes prevalence **11.4% ≈ 101 million**; prediabetes **15.3% ≈ 136 million**; hypertension **35.5% ≈ 315 million** — [Morung Express summary of ICMR-INDIAB](https://morungexpress.com/315-mn-indians-suffer-from-hypertension-101-mn-are-diabetic-icmr-study); [National Medical Journal of India commentary on ICMR-INDIAB](https://nmji.in/?p=6485); [ICMR IEC summary PDF](https://consumer-voice.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/07/ICMR-IEC.pdf)
- ICMR-INDIAB: hypertension, obesity and dyslipidaemia were all more common in urban than rural India; prediabetes was the exception (higher rurally). Diabetes highest in southern and northern regions, lower in central and north-eastern regions; Kerala, Puducherry, Goa, Sikkim and Punjab among the highest-burden states — [ICMR IEC summary PDF](https://consumer-voice.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/07/ICMR-IEC.pdf); [Morung Express](https://morungexpress.com/315-mn-indians-suffer-from-hypertension-101-mn-are-diabetic-icmr-study)
- ICMR-INDIAB authors state the burden is considerably higher than previously estimated, and that diabetes prevalence is stabilising in more developed states while still rising in most others — [ICMR IEC summary PDF](https://consumer-voice.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/07/ICMR-IEC.pdf)
- LASI (Longitudinal Ageing Study in India, wave 1) summary: **75% of elderly people suffer from one or another chronic disease**; prevalence rises with age — [ORF, Screen time may be rising steadily among older Indians](https://www.orfonline.org/expert-speak/screen-time-may-be-rising-steadily-among-older-indians) **[secondary source citing LASI]**

**Who is actually on medicines — the treatment cascade**
- NFHS-5 analysis, ages 15–49: hypertension prevalence 18.1%; of those with hypertension, **44.7% aware, 13.3% on treatment, 7.9% controlled**. Male sex, rural residence, lower household wealth and being unmarried were associated with greater losses at each cascade step — [Hypertension Control Cascade and Regional Performance in India, 2015–2021 (Cureus)](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC10042544/) **[snippet-level, verify in full text]**
- Same NFHS-4→NFHS-5 comparison: screening yield and treatment initiation improved in NFHS-5 versus NFHS-4, but control did not keep pace; authors describe the national hypertension control cascade as "largely ineffectual" — [Cureus 2023](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC10042544/)
- India Hypertension Control Initiative (IHCI), ~1 million people treated in public facilities across 15 states, 2018–2022: **~44% had BP under control**, ranging 26%–57% by facility type; Maharashtra, Punjab and Rajasthan above 50%, Nagaland, Jharkhand and Bihar below 25%; weakest states partly attributed to higher proportions of **missed visits** — [BMJ Open analysis reported via Science Chronicle](https://sciencechronicle.in/2025/12/04/india-hypertension-control-initiative-over-50-in-maharashtra-punjab-rajasthan-had-blood-pressure-under-control) **[secondary reporting of a 2025 BMJ Open paper]**
- IHCI protocol-based care, Punjab and Maharashtra (*Global Heart*, 2024): only **159,292 of 281,209 initiated patients returned for follow-up** — i.e. roughly 43% attrition before the first follow-up. Among those who did return, BP control at step 1 (amlodipine 5 mg) was 70% (Punjab, n=33,450 follow-ups) and 76% (Maharashtra, n=125,842); adding a second drug raised control to 75% and ~81% — [Global Heart 2024](https://doaj.org/article/0b0a140dda0f461ab3f51789f02ddfb2) **[snippet-level, verify in full text]**

**Pill counts / polypharmacy**
- Systematic review and meta-analysis of 27 Indian studies in older adults (*Frontiers in Pharmacology*, 2021): pooled **polypharmacy (5–9 medicines) 49% (95% CI 42–56)**; **hyperpolypharmacy (10+) 31%**; potentially inappropriate medication use 28% — [Frontiers in Pharmacology 2021](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC8173298/)
- Regional figures from the same review are internally inconsistent across versions: one reports highest polypharmacy in the North-east (65%), another reports 72% in North India (64–79) — [Frontiers in Pharmacology 2021](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fphar.2021.685518/full); conflicting figure noted in [ISPOR presentation abstract](https://www.ispor.org/heor-resources/presentations-database/presentation/intl2021-3339/110501). **Treat regional polypharmacy splits as unreliable.**
- Cross-sectional study, 310 patients aged 60+, Mangalore tertiary hospital (2023): polypharmacy more prevalent among female patients, older age groups and inpatients — [PMC12893539](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC12893539/)
- Indicative daily pill burden by population (small, non-comparable single-site studies): chronic kidney disease ~10–14 pills/day (n=244, Tirupati); diabetes with comorbidities ~4.6 pills/day; rural older adults ~4 drugs per prescription with ~30% on 5+ (n=207); Madhya Pradesh general prescribing average 2.8 drugs per prescription; South Indian community pharmacy data most commonly four drugs per prescription — compiled from [Journal of Pharmacy and Bioallied Sciences 2021](https://doaj.org/article/af86620cd78341d0bb40cf1eb8dfb604), [Journal of Family Medicine and Primary Care 2021](https://doaj.org/article/642d687a0e9d4b8cb106f44622771ea0), [WHO-indicator prescribing study, Dehradun](https://demodspace.chitkara.edu.in/handle/123456789/232) **[all snippet-level; small and non-representative]**

### Inferences
- The addressable market for a reminder app is **not** the 315 million with hypertension; it is the far smaller treated population plus the diabetes population (where treatment rates are higher because diabetes is symptomatic sooner). A realistic design target is someone on 2–5 daily medicines across 2–3 chronic conditions, not a single-drug user.
- The IHCI attrition figure (43% never returning for a first follow-up) and the NFHS-5 13%-treated figure suggest that **the dominant failure mode in India is not "missed a dose today" but "fell out of care entirely months ago."** A reminder app that only pings at dose times addresses the smaller problem. Refill and follow-up-visit prompting is likely to matter more.
- Polypharmacy at 49% in 60+ means a design that assumes a short medicine list will fail for the single most adherence-relevant demographic.

### Gaps
- **No national dataset distinguishing how many Indians take exactly 1 vs 2 vs 3+ daily medicines.** NFHS and LASI report prevalence and treatment status, not medicine counts. The polypharmacy literature only reports threshold-crossing (5+, 10+) in older adults, and only from clinic/hospital samples, which over-sample sicker patients.
- No national figure found for TB, thyroid or cardiac disease prevalence in the same comparable frame as ICMR-INDIAB; TB figures should be sourced from the India TB Report / WHO Global TB Report, which this research pass did not retrieve.
- NFHS-6 chronic-disease modules: a 2026 analysis referencing NFHS-6 was found for mobile ownership (see Q6) but no NFHS-6 hypertension/diabetes cascade figures surfaced.

## Q2. Measured medication adherence rates in India, and international comparison

### Takeaway
The best current pooled estimate is **39.3% non-adherence (95% CI 29.6–49.4%)** among Indian type 2 diabetes patients across 16 studies (n=4,170) published 2020–2024 — but with I²=97.3%, a prediction interval of 10.7%–76.5%, evidence of publication bias and GRADE certainty rated *very low*. Individual study estimates range from 11% to 68%. There is no all-condition national adherence meta-analysis for India.

### Cited Findings

**The headline meta-analysis (Basu, Sengupta & Kundu, published online 17 Feb 2026)**
- 16 studies, **n = 4,170**; pooled non-adherence **39.3% (95% CI 29.6–49.4%)**; range across studies **11%–68%** — [PMC12914141](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12914141/)
- MMAS-8 subgroup (4 studies, n=920): pooled non-adherence **48% (95% CI 18–79%)**, I²=94.4% — [PMC12914141](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12914141/)
- Heterogeneity across all studies: **I² = 97.3%**, τ²=0.0349, Q=551.50 (df 15), p<0.0001; **prediction interval 10.7%–76.5%**; Egger's test for publication bias p=0.027; **GRADE certainty: very low** — [PMC12914141](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12914141/)
- Per-study non-adherence (scale, n): Aravindakshan 2021 11% (self-designed 8-item, n=218); Lyngdoh 2021 15% (SDSCA, n=250); Sharma 2023 21% (Brief Medication Questionnaire, n=400); Kowsalya 2020 22% (MMAS-8, n=60); Udupa 2023 28% (MARS, n=101); Hulugappa 2022 38% (self-designed, n=70); Mishra 2023 38% (Hill-Bone HB-MAS, n=207); Olickal 2021 39% (Morisky-Green-Levine, n=1,002 — largest study); Prathap 2021 40% (MARS-5, n=150); Thapar 2020 43% (MMAS-8, n=124); Verma 2024 46% (MMAS-4, n=240); Sharma 2020 47% (Barriers to Insulin Treatment Questionnaire, n=225); Mishra 2021 56% (MARS, n=277); Ghosh 2022 60% (MMAS-8, n=165); Sahoo 2022 65% (MMAS-8, n=331); Singh 2021 68% (13-item questionnaire, n=350) — [PMC12914141, Table 2](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12914141/)
- Source discrepancies the review itself contains: the text gives a community-setting non-adherence range of 22–38% but Table 2 lists 11% and 15% for community studies; the Discussion says "all 14 studies" when 16 were included; the Verma (2024) injectable-drug CI is printed as 0.09–0.06 (upper bound below lower bound) — [PMC12914141](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12914141/). **Flagged as a data-quality warning for the report writer.**

**Hypertension-specific measured adherence**
- Kerala community survey, 139 adults (2016): adherence good in **49.6%**, moderate 31.7%, poor 18.7% — [Int J Res Med Sci / msjonline](https://www.msjonline.org/index.php/ijrms/article/view/3921) **[pre-2020 foundational]**
- Urban OPD study, 186 patients: adherence **68.3%** — [msjonline extract](https://www.msjonline.org/index.php/ijrms/article/download/3921/3462/16405) **[snippet-level]**
- A review cited within that study reports Indian hypertension adherence spanning roughly **43%–88%** across studies — [msjonline extract](https://www.msjonline.org/index.php/ijrms/article/download/3921/3462/16405) **[snippet-level]**
- 608 Indian patients (2011): non-adherence in **49.67%**; **54.93% said it is difficult to get a refill on time** — surfaced via [IHCI-related search extract](https://nicpd.ac.in/ojs-/index.php/gjmedph/article/download/4108/3198/7147) **[pre-2020 foundational; snippet-level]**

**Interventions evidence base**
- Systematic review of adherence interventions for NCDs in India (Tolley et al., *Frontiers in Public Health*, 2023; PROSPERO CRD42022345636): of 1,552 unique articles screened, **22 RCTs** met criteria — **12 education-based, 4 education + regular follow-up, only 2 technology-based** — [Frontiers in Public Health 2023](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/public-health/articles/10.3389/fpubh.2023.1194919/full); [PMC10311913](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC10311913/)

### Inferences
- The spread (11%–68%) is driven more by **instrument choice than by true population variation**: MMAS-8 studies cluster high (22%–65%) while self-designed instruments produce the lowest estimates (11%). Any adherence number quoted for India without naming the scale is close to meaningless. For app design, this means in-app "adherence scores" should be presented as a trend over time, not as a benchmarkable percentage.
- Only **2 of 22** Indian adherence RCTs tested a technology-based intervention. The Indian evidence base for digital adherence tools outside TB is close to empty — which is both the risk and the opportunity for the product.

### Gaps
- **No direct, like-for-like international comparison was retrieved.** The commonly cited WHO baseline (~50% adherence to long-term therapy in developed countries, from WHO's 2003 *Adherence to Long-Term Therapies* report) was not verified in this research pass and should not be quoted without checking the primary source.
- No pooled Indian adherence estimate covering hypertension, cardiac disease or thyroid; the only meta-analysis is diabetes-only.
- No pharmacy-refill-based (objective) adherence measure for India, e.g. proportion of days covered from claims data; all Indian estimates found are self-report scales, which systematically overestimate adherence.

## Q3. Documented reasons Indian patients miss doses

### Takeaway
Across Indian studies the same cluster recurs: **forgetfulness, cost/running out of stock, and deliberately stopping when symptoms resolve or the patient "feels well."** The relative ranking flips by setting — in urban slums cost dominates (73.8% of hypertensive patients citing lack of money), in better-off community samples forgetfulness dominates (70.5% in Kerala). Polypharmacy independently triples the odds of non-adherence.

### Cited Findings

**Patient-stated reasons, by setting**
- **Kerala, community survey (n=139):** forgetfulness **70.5%**, high medication cost **51.8%**, symptom-free state **27.3%** — [msjonline / Int J Res Med Sci](https://www.msjonline.org/index.php/ijrms/article/view/3921) **[pre-2020 foundational]**
- **Navi Mumbai urban slums (cross-sectional, 2017–18):** lack of money cited by **73.78% of hypertension patients and 50.58% of diabetes patients**; difficulty remembering due to work/forgetfulness by **26.21% of hypertension and 49.41% of diabetes patients** — [IJCMPH extract](https://www.ijcmph.com/index.php/ijcmph/article/download/817/701/2779) **[snippet-level]**
- **Delhi low-income area (30 in-depth interviews, qualitative):** main reasons were forgetfulness, carelessness, and **running out of drug stocks** — [Indian Journal of Public Health 2019](https://doaj.org/article/2dbdeb51d1e34c7a8bb4d8d82268528c) **[snippet-level]**
- **Urban OPD (n=186):** predominant reasons were **discontinuation of medicine on feeling well**, forgetfulness, and unawareness — [msjonline extract](https://www.msjonline.org/index.php/ijrms/article/download/3921/3462/16405) **[snippet-level]**
- **Rural Ludhiana, Punjab (n=184):** most frequent reason was **discontinuation when feeling well**, followed by forgetfulness and financial issues — [JCDR](https://www.jcdr.net/articles/PDF/11345/31867_271117_31867_F(P)_PF1(MLS_AnG)_PFA(MLS_AnG)_PB(MLS_AnG)_PN(AP).pdf) **[snippet-level]**

**Statistically modelled predictors (single-study estimates, vote-counted not pooled, from the 2026 diabetes meta-analysis)**
- Sometimes forgetting medication: **OR 25.16 (6.78–93.28)** (Hulugappa 2022) — [PMC12914141](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12914141/)
- **Polypharmacy: OR 3.15 (1.73–5.75)** (Verma 2024) — [PMC12914141](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12914141/)
- **Absence of side effects: AOR 2.18 (1.02–4.61)** (Thapar 2020) — i.e. patients who feel nothing are *more* likely to stop — [PMC12914141](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12914141/)
- Comorbidity AOR 3.26 (1.93–5.50); family history AOR 1.89 (1.11–3.17); alcohol AOR 2.36 (1.03–5.36) (Sahoo 2022) — [PMC12914141](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12914141/)
- Anxiety AOR 3.52 (1.62–7.61); current smoker AOR 5.47 (1.56–19.24); regular physical activity protective AOR 0.311 (0.12–0.79) (Mishra 2023) — [PMC12914141](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12914141/)
- Female sex APR 1.44 (1.19–1.74); **daily wage worker APR 1.70 (1.14–2.54)**; tobacco use APR 1.46 (1.16–1.84); **distance to health facility APR 1.21 (1.04–1.41)** (Olickal 2021, n=1,002 — the largest included study) — [PMC12914141](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12914141/)
- Older age OR 6.13 (2.25–17.73) and lower socio-economic status OR 0.20 (0.05–0.71) (Aravindakshan 2021); older age OR 1.80 (1.10–2.90), longer diabetes duration OR 1.80 (1.00–3.20), knowledge OR 1.80 (1.10–3.10) (Sharma 2023) — [PMC12914141](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12914141/)
- Insulin-specific barriers (qualitative, Sharma 2020, n=225): low self-efficacy, doubts about clinical benefit, fear of hypoglycaemia, needle phobia — [PMC12914141](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12914141/)
- The review's own note: reasons such as **carelessness, fear of side effects and "feeling better" were not quantified in any included study** — a documented hole in the Indian evidence base — [PMC12914141](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12914141/)

**Cost as a structural driver**
- National Health Accounts 2022-23: pharmacy spending **₹1.63 lakh crore = 21.2% of current health expenditure**; household out-of-pocket spending **43.4% of total health expenditure** in 2022-23, down from 64.2% in 2013-14 — [Medical Dialogues reporting NHA 2022-23](https://medicaldialogues.in/news/industry/pharma/india-spent-over-rs-16-lakh-crore-on-medicines-in-2022-2023-reveals-nha-report-172268)
- NHSRC: medicines are **the single largest component of out-of-pocket payments, approximately 63%** — [NHSRC, Identification of Factors Contributing to OOPE on Medicines](https://nhsrcindia.org/sites/default/files/2025-08/Identification%20of%20Factors%20Contributing%20to%20OOPE%20on%20Medicines.pdf)
- Contradicted by a JGU/PGIMER exit-interview study (5,252 OPD patients, three states, 2022), which puts the medicines share of OOP expenditure at **~30%** (30.74% patient-reported vs 29.61% imputation-based for organised private hospitals) — [JGU repository](https://pure.jgu.edu.in/id/eprint/4726/). The two figures cover different populations and definitions and should not be averaged.

**TB-specific barriers (measured, not self-reported)**
- Cohort of **650 Indian TB patients** tested with urine isoniazid assays during **unannounced home visits**: **structural barriers to collecting medication refills and psychosocial barriers emerged as the most important factors** contributing to non-adherence; missing doses during drug-susceptible TB treatment is associated with a large increase in the odds of unfavourable outcomes — [Subbaraman et al., medRxiv preprint 2021](https://www.medrxiv.org/content/10.1101/2021.01.12.21249665v1.full.pdf) **[preprint, not peer-reviewed as retrieved; full text returned HTTP 403, findings are from search extract]**

### Inferences
- **"Feeling well" is not a minor reason — it may be the modal reason for hypertension specifically.** Two separate studies (Punjab rural, urban OPD) rank it first, and Thapar 2020 shows the absence of side effects *doubles* the odds of non-adherence. An app that only reminds does nothing for this; an app that explains "your BP is controlled *because* of this tablet" targets the actual mechanism.
- **Running out of stock is a distinct failure mode from forgetting**, and appears in the Delhi qualitative work and the 2011 refill study (54.93% found timely refill difficult) and in the TB biomarker cohort (structural refill barriers as the top factor). Refill countdown and reorder prompting is probably higher-yield than dose reminders.
- The daily-wage-worker predictor (APR 1.70) and the Navi Mumbai cost figure point to a segment where the binding constraint is money and clinic-visit opportunity cost, not memory. A reminder app cannot solve this and should not be designed as if it can.

### Gaps
- **No Indian quantitative study found on distrust of allopathic medicine as a measured cause of non-adherence**, nor on "pill fatigue" as a named construct with a prevalence figure.
- **AYUSH switching: no evidence found that Indian patients commonly abandon allopathic chronic-disease medicines for AYUSH.** The literature retrieved frames AYUSH as an *add-on* to standard care in the NPCDCS-AYUSH integration project (Rajasthan, Bihar, Gujarat), which observed dose reduction/discontinuation of conventional medicines as a supervised outcome, not as patient-initiated abandonment — [Journal of Ayurveda and Integrative Medicine 2021](https://doaj.org/article/fcb4b89c286044df9f1d8557b1f64755); the retrieved extract cut off before the discontinuation rate. A qualitative study notes lack of scientific evidence is the main barrier to AYUSH becoming mainstream for diabetes — [Int J General Medicine (Dovepress)](https://www.dovepress.com/role-of-ayush-therapies-in-modern-medicine-a-qualitative-study-to-expl-peer-reviewed-article-IJGM). **The report should not assert AYUSH-driven non-adherence without better evidence.**
- No study retrieved quantifying side effects as a stated reason with a percentage, despite its prominence in clinical commentary.

## Q4. Role of family caregivers; who manages medicines for an elderly parent

### Takeaway
This is the weakest-evidenced area in the entire research pass. **I found essentially no peer-reviewed Indian research quantifying who manages medicines for elderly parents in Indian households.** The India-specific material is almost entirely commercial elder-care blog content, and the one peer-reviewed Indian finding concerns psychiatric patients, not general geriatric care.

### Cited Findings
- Peer-reviewed, India, but **mental-illness-specific**: when people with mental illness refuse medicines, the burden of ensuring intake usually falls on family members, and caregivers have sometimes administered medicines **covertly** — [PMC9707656](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9707656). This is an important and genuinely India-documented dynamic, but it does not generalise to diabetes/hypertension care without further evidence.
- Indian elder-care service provider (Samarth, **commercial source, not research**): older adults often stop medicines without telling family; side effects are a common reason; some quietly drop a drug they feel "doesn't suit them"; complicated schedules are another frequent cause of missed doses — [Samarth Community](https://care.samarth.community/?p=130663)
- Same source: support for medication routines in Indian families comes from a neighbour, a community nurse, or a **family WhatsApp group** — [Samarth Community](https://care.samarth.community/blog/safety/understanding-polypharmacy-how-you-can-help-your-parents-manage-multiple-medications-safely/)
- A guide aimed at NRI children of elderly Indian parents (**commercial source, no underlying studies named**) claims over half of elderly Indians with diabetes and hypertension do not consistently take their medicines, and that polypharmacy affects over one in three elderly urban residents; it also observes that prescription instructions are **scattered across chits rather than kept in one place**, and proposes remote dose verification — [TechBullion](https://techbullion.com/?p=303486). **These numbers are uncited and should not be reported as fact.**
- Non-India comparison baseline: a Finnish peer-reviewed study of family caregivers of older adults on multiple prescriptions frames medication management as a demanding task that adds to caregiver burden — [University of Turku research portal](https://research.utu.fi/converis/portal/detail/Publication/499380209) **[international comparison only]**
- Indirect supporting signal from Q6: among older Indian cancer patients, 81% used mobile phones but only 25% accessed the internet, with lower use among those with no education or impaired cognition — [ORF citing the study](https://www.orfonline.org/expert-speak/screen-time-may-be-rising-steadily-among-older-indians). This implies a large group of chronically ill elderly who have a phone but cannot operate an app independently.

### Inferences
- The **absence** of research is itself a finding worth reporting: the "adult child manages parent's medicines" model is widely assumed in Indian product design but is not empirically quantified in the literature I could reach. The report should say so plainly rather than inventing a figure.
- The convergent circumstantial case for a caregiver-centric design is: 49% polypharmacy in 60+, only 13% of 60+ having ever used the internet (see Q6), 81% phone use but 25% internet use among elderly patients, and the documented pattern of parents silently stopping medicines. That combination argues the *primary app user* is plausibly the adult child and the *patient* is a secondary or non-user — but this is an inference from adjacent data, not a measured finding.
- The covert-administration finding from psychiatric care is a caution: a product feature that lets a family member monitor doses without the patient's knowledge has a documented cultural precedent in India and a correspondingly real consent problem.

### Gaps
- **No Indian survey found measuring: what proportion of elderly patients have a family member who fills their pillbox, orders refills, or accompanies them to appointments; whether that person is typically a daughter, daughter-in-law, son, or spouse; or how this differs by urban/rural and by joint vs nuclear household.** This is the single largest evidence gap for the product.
- No Indian data on remote/NRI caregiving prevalence for medication management.
- No Indian data on the division of labour between spouse-caregivers (who co-age and may themselves be on polypharmacy) and adult-child caregivers.

## Q5. Role of the chemist/pharmacist; handwritten vs typed prescriptions; how patients know what to take

### Takeaway
Indian community pharmacy is overwhelmingly a dispensing trade rather than a counselling profession — ~0.6 million community pharmacists, most holding only a D.Pharm, functioning as "prescription fillers." Counselling is widely believed by pharmacists to be their duty but is rarely delivered, blocked by doctor-dispensing, lack of training and weak legal backing. **I found no study quantifying handwritten vs typed prescriptions in India, nor any study measuring whether patients identify their medicines by strip, prescription paper, or memory.**

### Cited Findings
- Survey-based review: "the majority of practicing pharmacists are confined to trade activities in the community and engage only in filling the prescriptions"; approximately **0.6 million pharmacists serving in the community, most with a basic qualification of Diploma in Pharmacy** — [Indian J Pharmaceutical Sciences](https://www.ijpsonline.com/articles/attitudes-and-behaviors-of-practicing-community-pharmacists-towards-patient-counselling.pdf)
- Karnataka survey of **258 community pharmacists** (2009): **80% agreed patient counselling is their professional obligation**, yet the authors concluded pharmacists "are remaining as the prescription fillers rather than acting as the link between patients and prescribers" — [Indian J Pharmaceutical Sciences](https://www.ijpsonline.com/articles/attitudes-and-behaviors-of-practicing-community-pharmacists-towards-patient-counselling.pdf) **[pre-2020 foundational]**
- Karnataka vs Kerala comparison: Karnataka pharmacists viewed counselling as a shared doctor–pharmacist responsibility; Kerala pharmacists viewed it as the pharmacist's responsibility. Main barriers across both: **doctor dispensing, lack of knowledge, and non-legalisation of patient counselling** — [IJPS, two-state study](https://www.ijpsonline.com/abstract/patient-counseling-practicing-community-pharmacists-perceptions-from-two-south-indian-states-606.html) **[pre-2020 foundational]**
- 2016 thesis on Indian community pharmacies: previously "the majority of pharmacies had non-pharmacists dispensing or counseling patients"; Pharm.D graduates' presence has made only minimal progress; recommends making Pharm.D mandatory for dispensing pharmacists — [Eastern Michigan University thesis](https://commons.emich.edu/theses/851) **[pre-2020 foundational]**
- Under the **Pharmacy Practice Regulations 2021**, a qualified clinical pharmacist is responsible for patient counselling — cited in a 2025 paper — [Indian J Pharmaceutical Education and Research](https://ijper.org/sites/default/files/IndJPhaEdRes-59-1s-264.pdf)
- A quasi-experimental study in an Indian tertiary care hospital tested a clinical pharmacist counselling programme for chronic illness patients, measuring adherence with the Morisky scale plus knowledge/attitudes/practices and satisfaction pre- and post-intervention — [msjonline](https://www.msjonline.org/index.php/ijrms/article/view/17044); **outcome results were not available in the retrieved abstract.**
- Indirect: South Indian community pharmacy prescription data most commonly showed **four drugs per prescription** — [search extract, source unverified] **[snippet-level, low confidence]**
- Indirect on prescription handling: an elder-care commercial source observes that Indian families keep prescription instructions "scattered across chits rather than kept in one place" — [TechBullion](https://techbullion.com/?p=303486) **[commercial source, not research]**
- Structural relevance: distance to health facility was a significant predictor of non-adherence (APR 1.21, 1.04–1.41) in the largest Indian diabetes adherence study (Olickal 2021, n=1,002) — [PMC12914141](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12914141/), which makes the nearby chemist the de facto point of contact between clinic visits.

### Inferences
- Because the pharmacist does not counsel and the prescription is a handwritten chit, **the medicine strip itself is the most likely primary artefact the patient actually uses to identify what to take.** This is an inference, not a measured finding, but it is consistent with the dispensing-only model and with the absence of any standardised patient-facing label. It argues for strip-photo / blister-pack-image-based medicine entry in the app rather than typed drug names.
- Pharmacists are a structurally available but professionally unused channel: 80% say counselling is their job, almost none do it. Any pharmacy-integration strategy is building on willingness that already exists but has no workflow.

### Gaps
- **No study found on the prevalence of handwritten vs typed/printed prescriptions in India**, in either public or private sector, despite this being directly load-bearing for an app's data-entry design.
- **No study found measuring how patients identify their medicines** (strip vs prescription vs memory vs caregiver). This is a genuine hole — the only material I found on this is a commercial elder-care blog.
- No recent (post-2020) national survey of community pharmacist counselling behaviour; the quantitative surveys are from 2009 and 2016.
- No data on how often the chemist, rather than the doctor, is the one who tells a patient what to take or substitutes a generic.

## Q6. Age, smartphone ownership and chronic conditions

### Takeaway
Phone *access* in older Indian households is high (84–94%) but *internet* use among those 60+ is only ~13%, and among older patients with illness, phone use (81%) vastly exceeds internet use (25%). Women's personal mobile use was 54% in NFHS-5, rising to a reported 63.6% in NFHS-6 — and only 33% in the poorest wealth quintile. **No dataset was found that directly cross-tabulates smartphone ownership against chronic disease status.**

### Cited Findings
- **LASI (via ORF analysis):** mobile phones are the most prevalent consumer durable in Indian households with a member aged 45+; access **93.6% urban vs 84.1% rural** — [ORF](https://www.orfonline.org/expert-speak/screen-time-may-be-rising-steadily-among-older-indians) **[secondary source citing LASI]**
- **NITI Aayog (via ORF):** only **13% of Indians aged 60+ have ever used the internet** — far below national internet penetration — [ORF](https://www.orfonline.org/expert-speak/screen-time-may-be-rising-steadily-among-older-indians)
- **Older Indian cancer patients:** **81% used mobile phones but only 25% accessed the internet**; use was lower among those with no education and those with impaired cognition — [ORF, citing the study](https://www.orfonline.org/expert-speak/screen-time-may-be-rising-steadily-among-older-indians). This is the closest available proxy for "chronically ill older adult" digital capability.
- **NFHS-5 (2019–21):** **54% of women personally use a mobile phone**, up from 45.9% in NFHS-4. Range by state: 39% (Madhya Pradesh) and 41% (Chhattisgarh) to 91% (Goa), 89% (Sikkim), 87% (Kerala). By wealth: **33% in the lowest quintile vs 79% in the highest** — [Down To Earth analysis of NFHS-5](https://www.downtoearth.org.in/economy/what-does-nfhs-5-data-tell-us-about-state-of-women-empowerment-in-india-80920)
- **NFHS-6:** women's mobile ownership reported at **63.6%**, against the NFHS-5 baseline of 54% — [Factly analysis, 2026, cited in search results](https://insights.dataful.in/articles/womens-internet-use-nearly-doubles-but-access-gaps-remain) **[secondary analysis; verify against the NFHS-6 factsheet before publication]**
- **mHealth awareness:** a survey of adults aged 35–65 in Madhya Pradesh found awareness of chronic disease management apps was only **19.4%** — [Population Medicine](https://www.populationmedicine.eu/Awareness-and-Use-of-Mobile-Health-Applications-Among-Middle-Aged-Adults-in-Madhya,230242,0,2.html)

### Inferences
- The gap between **phone access (84–94%)** and **internet use among 60+ (13%)** is the single most important design constraint surfaced in this research. It means the intervention that reaches the largest number of elderly Indian patients is not an app at all — it is a **voice call or SMS to a feature phone**, which is precisely the design logic of 99DOTS (see Q7).
- The 54%→63.6% jump in women's mobile use between NFHS-5 and NFHS-6, combined with 33% ownership in the poorest quintile, means a female patient in a low-income household is likely to be reachable only via a household member's phone. Multi-patient profiles on a single device is a requirement, not a nice-to-have.
- 19.4% awareness of chronic-disease apps among the 35–65 cohort means the app has to be introduced by a human — a doctor, a chemist, or an adult child — rather than discovered.

### Gaps
- **No source found that cross-tabulates smartphone ownership (as distinct from any mobile phone) by chronic disease status or by medication count.** LASI reports household durable ownership, not individual smartphone ownership by disease.
- No Indian data found on what proportion of chronic-disease patients rely on a *family member's* phone rather than their own — the question as posed cannot be answered from available sources.
- LASI wave 1 primary reports were not fetched directly in this pass; all LASI figures here come from ORF's secondary analysis and should be checked against the [MoHFW LASI brief](https://www.mohfw.gov.in/sites/default/files/LASI%20Brief_Final.pdf) and the [IIPS LASI executive summary](https://iipsindia.ac.in/sites/default/files/LASI_India_Executive_Summary_0.pdf).

## Q7. TB adherence programmes — what India learned from 99DOTS, MERM boxes and video DOT

### Takeaway
India's digital adherence experiments produced a consistent and uncomfortable lesson: **the technologies measure a proxy (a phone call, a box opening, a video), not ingestion, and when validated against a urine biomarker they are inaccurate — largely because patients do not engage with them.** The largest 99DOTS evaluation found essentially no population-level change in favourable outcomes (93.1% pre vs 92.9% post); the apparent benefit is confined to patients who engaged, which is self-selection rather than effect.

### Cited Findings

**99DOTS (toll-free call from a number hidden under the blister pack)**
- **Himachal Pradesh pre-post study (BMC Infectious Diseases, 2023)**, using national TB database records: pre-intervention cohort initiated treatment Feb–Oct 2017 (**N = 7,722**); post-intervention cohort Jul 2018–Mar 2019 (**N = 8,322**). Overall favourable outcomes were **93.1% pre vs 92.9% post** — essentially unchanged. Within the post period, patients who called 99DOTS had a favourable outcome rate of **94.8% vs 92.0% for those who never called** — [BMC Infectious Diseases 2023, doi 10.1186/s12879-023-08418-2](https://doi.org/10.1186/s12879-023-08418-2); [PMC10391893](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC10391893/) **[figures from search extract; the PMC page returned a CAPTCHA and the Springer mirror required authentication, so the adjusted regression estimate could not be retrieved — verify in full text]**
- **Accuracy validation (Khandewale, Subbaraman et al.):** in **597 patients**, 99DOTS records were compared against urine isoniazid tests collected at **unannounced home visits**; the authors concluded 99DOTS has **suboptimal accuracy for measuring adherence, partly due to poor patient engagement with 99DOTS** — [medRxiv preprint](https://www.medrxiv.org/content/10.1101/19011197.full.pdf)
- Subbaraman has separately criticised reliance on adherence gadgets, finding that one widely used pill pack **underreported missed pills for many patients and overreported them for others** — [Tufts Now](https://now.tufts.edu/2022/05/15/better-approaches-fighting-tb-india)
- **Rajkot, Gujarat evaluation (Lung India, 2019):** 347 registered patients, Feb 2016–Sep 2017, assessing adherence rate and treatment outcomes — [Lung India 2019](https://doaj.org/article/22a9f31c49de4c02a834dddbb900bba3) **[enrolment profile only retrieved; outcome findings not obtained]**

**MERM (Medication Event Reminder Monitor / evriMED500 smart pillbox)**
- **ISRCTN93928296** — three-arm individually randomised trial in India (1:1:1, stratified by health-care-worker and facility type, public vs private) comparing **99DLite toll-free calling** and **MERM smart pillboxes** against **refill monitoring** as control; primary objective is relative impact on TB treatment adherence and outcomes; ethics approvals from Dayanand Medical College (Ludhiana) and the Indian School of Business (Hyderabad) — [ISRCTN93928296](https://www.isrctn.com/ISRCTN93928296). **No published results retrieved.**
- **NCT04216420** — MERM-observed self-administered therapy vs standard daily DOT; MERM arm (n=57) receives a 15-day TB medication supply in the evriMED500 device; **total enrolment 114 (actual)**; both arms validated with urine isoniazid testing — [ClinicalTrials.gov NCT04216420](https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT04216420). **No published results retrieved.**
- Mechanism limitation stated in the literature: MERM "compiles dosing histories by capturing data on pillbox opening **as a proxy for dose ingestion**," which is why it cannot confirm a pill was swallowed — [JMIR 2021, MERM pilot in India's MDR-TB programme](https://www.jmir.org/2021/6/e23294)
- A Research Square preprint notes **MERM risks over-reporting or under-reporting in the same way as 99DOTS, because both depend primarily on patient behaviour** — [Research Square preprint](https://www.researchsquare.com/article/rs-1727814/v1) **[preprint, not peer-reviewed]**

**Video DOT (vDOT)**
- Pune single-arm prospective feasibility study: assessed adherence as the proportion of prescribed doses observed by video, plus acceptability — [JMIR Formative Research 2019 / PMC6734854](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC6734854/) **[pre-2020 foundational; outcome percentages not retrieved]**
- South India cohort feasibility and acceptability pilot of vDOT for antitubercular treatment — [PMC10230861](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC10230861/) **[feasibility/acceptability only; not an effectiveness trial]**

**Underlying problem these programmes were built to solve**
- Subbaraman et al., *PLOS Medicine* 2016 systematic review and meta-analysis of India's public-sector TB care cascade: approximately **half a million TB patients in India reach government TB diagnostic facilities but are either not successfully diagnosed or not started on treatment**; the authors also note India's programme reports only treatment *completion*, not recurrence-free survival — [PLOS Medicine 2016 (via Prolekare mirror)](https://www.prolekare.cz/casopisy/plos-medicine/2016-10/the-tuberculosis-cascade-of-care-in-indias-public-sector-a-systematic-review-and-meta-analysis-59374); [Springer Nature Communities author post](https://communities.springernature.com/posts/india-needs-to-address-gaps-in-the-tb-care-cascade) **[pre-2020 foundational]**
- Biomarker cohort (n=650): **structural barriers to collecting medication refills and psychosocial barriers were the most important contributors to non-adherence**, and missing doses in drug-susceptible TB treatment is associated with a large increase in odds of unfavourable outcomes — [Subbaraman et al., medRxiv 2021](https://www.medrxiv.org/content/10.1101/2021.01.12.21249665v1.full.pdf)

### Inferences
- **What worked:** the *distribution* model. 99DOTS deliberately required nothing more than a feature phone and a free call, which is the only reason it scaled nationally in a population where 60+ internet use is 13%. Any app targeting the same patients inherits a far narrower reach.
- **What did not work:** treating the engagement signal as an adherence measurement. The 93.1%→92.9% result means the technology did not move population outcomes; the 94.8% vs 92.0% split between callers and non-callers is almost certainly confounded, because the kind of patient who calls daily is the kind who takes pills daily. **The report should explicitly warn against citing that 2.8-point gap as an effect of the technology.**
- **The deepest lesson is diagnostic, not technical:** Subbaraman's biomarker work found the top barrier was *structural refill access*, not memory. Every Indian digital adherence tool built so far optimises for memory. This is the clearest evidence-based argument in the whole research pass that a reminder-first product is solving the second-order problem.
- Both MERM and 99DOTS fail the same way — they capture an action adjacent to swallowing — so a consumer app's self-reported "taken" tap is, at best, no more accurate than either, and should be presented to users as a log rather than as proof.

### Gaps
- **The adjusted regression estimate from the Himachal Pradesh 99DOTS study could not be retrieved** (PMC served a CAPTCHA; the Springer mirror required authentication). The 93.1%/92.9% and 94.8%/92.0% figures are from a search extract of the abstract and should be verified in the full text before publication.
- **No published results found for either randomised MERM trial in India** (ISRCTN93928296, NCT04216420) — both registry entries were retrieved but no outcome papers.
- No retrieved figure for what proportion of 99DOTS-enrolled patients never called at all (the "poor patient engagement" finding is qualitative in the extracts available).
- Nikshay (India's national TB case-management system) specifically: **no evaluation retrieved** in this pass. Nikshay appears only indirectly via the national TB database used in the Himachal study.
- No vDOT effectiveness results (only feasibility/acceptability studies were found for India).
- WHO South-East Asia regional reports were not retrieved in this pass.

## Cross-cutting source-quality warnings for the report writer

- The 2026 diabetes adherence meta-analysis ([PMC12914141](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12914141/)) is the single most useful quantitative source here, but it **self-rates its evidence as GRADE "very low"**, shows significant publication bias (Egger p=0.027), has I²=97.3%, and contains at least three internal inconsistencies (community-setting range, "14 studies" vs 16 included, an inverted confidence interval). Quote its individual study table with more confidence than its pooled estimate.
- Several high-value sources were **inaccessible**: PMC served a CAPTCHA for the 99DOTS Himachal paper, medRxiv returned HTTP 403 for the Subbaraman adherence preprint PDF, and Springer required authentication. Figures attributed to those papers here come from search-engine extracts of their abstracts.
- The two figures for medicines as a share of out-of-pocket health spending (**~63%** per NHSRC vs **~30%** per the JGU/PGIMER exit-interview study) **genuinely conflict** and cover different populations and methods.
- Regional polypharmacy figures from the 2021 Frontiers meta-analysis **conflict between versions of the same review** (65% North-east vs 72% North India).
- Q4 (family caregivers) and the handwritten-prescription and medicine-identification parts of Q5 are supported mainly by **commercial elder-care marketing content**, not research. The report should state that the evidence does not exist rather than dress up blog claims as findings.
