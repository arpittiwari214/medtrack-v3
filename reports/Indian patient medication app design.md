# Rebuild MedTrack around disengagement, not forgetting

**The strongest evidence in this research says the product is aimed at the wrong failure.** In India the dominant way medication fails is not a dose forgotten at 8pm — it is a patient who stopped attending, stopped refilling, or decided they were cured. India's largest protocol-based hypertension programme saw only **159,292 of 281,209 initiated patients return for even a first follow-up visit** ([Global Heart 2024](https://doaj.org/article/0b0a140dda0f461ab3f51789f02ddfb2)), NFHS-5 found only **13.3% of adults aged 15–49 with hypertension were on treatment at all** ([Cureus 2023](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC10042544/)), and the one Indian cohort that validated adherence against a urine biomarker rather than self-report concluded that **structural barriers to collecting refills, not memory, were the most important contributor to non-adherence** ([Subbaraman et al., medRxiv 2021](https://www.medrxiv.org/content/10.1101/2021.01.12.21249665v1.full.pdf)). India's national digital adherence experiment, 99DOTS, moved population outcomes from **93.1% to 92.9%** ([BMC Infectious Diseases 2023](https://doi.org/10.1186/s12879-023-08418-2)) — which is to say, not at all. Meanwhile the reminder engine MedTrack already ships is, on the most common Indian phone configuration, probably not firing on time: it declares `SCHEDULE_EXACT_ALARM` but never requests or checks it, on an installed base where roughly **68% of users are on API 31+** and Android 14+ **denies that permission by default on fresh installs** ([Android 14 changes](https://developer.android.com/about/versions/14/changes/schedule-exact-alarms)), and it holds a 50-notification horizon that runs dry in about three days for a polypharmacy patient who — by design — never opens the app. On the legal side the news is good and dull: **nothing in the DPDP Rules binds this app's product behaviour until roughly 13 May 2027**, a pure reminder-and-log tool is defensibly outside CDSCO's July 2026 medical-device-software guidance provided it never recommends anything, and the only piece of ABDM worth touching this year is the National Drug Registry. The report below separates what is broken, what the evidence says to change, and what is merely aspiration — and it is explicit about the several places where the evidence is too thin to carry a recommendation at all.

## India's treatment cascade leaks long before the 8pm dose

The headline numbers everyone cites for India are prevalence numbers: **101 million people with diabetes, 136 million with prediabetes, 315 million with hypertension** ([ICMR-INDIAB via ICMR IEC summary](https://consumer-voice.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/07/ICMR-IEC.pdf)). They are the wrong denominator for this product. The population actually on daily medicines is a fraction of that, because the cascade leaks at every step: of adults aged 15–49 with hypertension, **44.7% were aware, 13.3% on treatment and 7.9% controlled** ([Cureus 2023](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC10042544/)), and the authors describe the national control cascade as largely ineffectual. The India Hypertension Control Initiative, treating around a million people across 15 states, achieved **~44% BP control**, with the weakest states attributed partly to **missed visits** ([BMJ Open analysis via Science Chronicle](https://sciencechronicle.in/2025/12/04/india-hypertension-control-initiative-over-50-in-maharashtra-punjab-rajasthan-had-blood-pressure-under-control)). The attrition figure is the one that should reframe the product: in Punjab and Maharashtra, roughly **43% of initiated patients never came back for a first follow-up** ([Global Heart 2024](https://doaj.org/article/0b0a140dda0f461ab3f51789f02ddfb2)).

The qualitative reasons line up with that structural picture rather than with a memory story. In rural Ludhiana and in an urban OPD sample, the single most frequent reason patients gave was **discontinuing the medicine on feeling well** ([JCDR](https://www.jcdr.net/articles/PDF/11345/31867_271117_31867_F(P)_PF1(MLS_AnG)_PFA(MLS_AnG)_PB(MLS_AnG)_PN(AP).pdf); [Int J Res Med Sci](https://www.msjonline.org/index.php/ijrms/article/download/3921/3462/16405)), and one modelled estimate found the **absence of side effects roughly doubled the odds of non-adherence (AOR 2.18, 1.02–4.61)** ([PMC12914141](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12914141/)). Running out of stock appears as a distinct failure mode in Delhi qualitative work ([Indian Journal of Public Health 2019](https://doaj.org/article/2dbdeb51d1e34c7a8bb4d8d82268528c)), in an older refill study where **54.93% said timely refill was difficult** ([extract](https://nicpd.ac.in/ojs-/index.php/gjmedph/article/download/4108/3198/7147)), and at the top of the TB biomarker cohort's list. Cost is the binding constraint for a whole segment: in Navi Mumbai slums, **73.78% of hypertensive patients cited lack of money**, against 26.21% citing forgetting ([IJCMPH](https://www.ijcmph.com/index.php/ijcmph/article/download/817/701/2779)); among 1,002 diabetes patients, **being a daily wage worker (APR 1.70) and distance to the health facility (APR 1.21) were independent predictors** ([PMC12914141](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12914141/)).

**The counter-case deserves a hearing, because it is not weak.** Forgetfulness was the top-cited reason in a Kerala community survey at **70.5%** ([Int J Res Med Sci](https://www.msjonline.org/index.php/ijrms/article/view/3921)), and one modelled estimate puts the odds ratio for "sometimes forgetting" at **25.16 (6.78–93.28)** ([PMC12914141](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12914141/)). The ranking also flips by setting — cost dominates in slums, forgetting dominates in better-off community samples. But both of those are self-reported reasons, and forgetting is the most socially acceptable thing to tell an interviewer; when Indian TB patients were tested with unannounced urine assays instead of asked, structural refill access came out on top. The resolution is not that reminders are useless. It is that **reminders are the feature that serves the best-off segment of the addressable population, and MedTrack has already built the features that serve the rest — and buried them.**

That last point is the actionable one. MedTrack already models `stock` with `unitsRemaining`, `unitsPerDose`, `lowThreshold` and `lastRefillAt`, and already ships an Appointments screen. Those are not accessories to a reminder app; on this evidence they *are* the product. The decisions that follow: promote a days-of-supply countdown to the home surface rather than firing a one-shot low-stock alert; make a missed or overdue follow-up appointment a first-class, persistent state rather than a list row; and add a per-medicine `purpose` field the patient fills in their own words, because the "I feel fine, so I stopped" mechanism is addressed by the medicine having a visible reason to exist, and by nothing else in the app. Note the constraint from the legal section before writing any copy around this: an explanation of *why the patient was given a medicine* is fine; an app-generated statement that their condition is controlled, or that they should change timing, is not.

One number to avoid. The widely repeated claim that 99DOTS callers had better outcomes than non-callers (**94.8% vs 92.0%**) is almost certainly confounded — the patient who makes a free call every day is the patient who swallows a pill every day. The honest reading of that study is the population figure, 93.1% before and 92.9% after. Do not let the 2.8-point gap into a pitch deck.

## Who holds the phone is a different question from who takes the pill

The two research notes appear to contradict each other on the primary user, and the contradiction has to be settled before any screen is redesigned.

The caregiver case: only **13% of Indians aged 60+ have ever used the internet** ([NITI Aayog via ORF](https://www.orfonline.org/expert-speak/screen-time-may-be-rising-steadily-among-older-indians)), while household mobile access among those 45+ runs **93.6% urban and 84.1% rural**; among older Indian cancer patients, **81% used mobile phones but only 25% accessed the internet**, with use lower among those with no education or impaired cognition (same source). Pooled **polypharmacy of 5–9 medicines reaches 49% in Indian older adults** ([Frontiers in Pharmacology 2021](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC8173298/)), so the most adherence-relevant demographic is the one least able to operate an app. Awareness of chronic-disease management apps among adults aged 35–65 in one Madhya Pradesh survey was **19.4%** ([Population Medicine](https://www.populationmedicine.eu/Awareness-and-Use-of-Mobile-Health-Applications-Among-Middle-Aged-Adults-in-Madhya,230242,0,2.html)) — this app will be installed by someone, not discovered.

The self-management case is a single, much better study for this specific question: a questionnaire survey of **901 Indian cardiovascular patients** across nine cities found **89.7% self-managed their medication and only 10.3% were caregiver-managed** — in a sample where **39.2% were over 60 and 42.5% had less than high-school education** ([IJCDW 2026](https://ijcdw.org/survey-on-the-impact-of-pill-color-on-medication-adherence/)).

**These findings do not actually conflict; they measure different verbs.** "Self-manages medication" means deciding and swallowing. "Has never used the internet" means operating a smartphone. A 68-year-old woman who fills her own pillbox every Sunday and has never opened an app is counted in both figures, and she is the modal Indian chronic patient. The IJCDW sample is also selected: these are patients who reached a cardiology OPD and completed a questionnaire, and the measure is self-reported, in a culture where admitting that your daughter manages your pills is a statement about your competence. So the defensible synthesis is: **most Indian patients manage their own medicines, and a large minority of those same patients cannot run the app that would help them.** The app user and the patient are frequently different people without the patient being a passive recipient of care.

That resolves the design question without picking a side. The pattern that fits is **CoWIN's**, not Medisafe's: CoWIN let one person enrol up to five others and pick different centres for each, which is the single most transferable decision in India's digital public infrastructure ([Harvard case 9-724-371](https://ten.nl/wp-content/uploads/2025/05/Harvard-Case.pdf)). MedTrack's current `CareLink` model is the wrong shape for this. It is an email invitation that creates a second account and grants a remote observer scoped read permissions — the NRI-adult-child model, which is the least evidenced of the three. What the data argues for is **several patient profiles on one phone, under one login**, because the daughter-in-law's phone holds her own, her husband's and her mother-in-law's medicines, and because women's personal mobile use was only **33% in the poorest wealth quintile** against 79% in the richest ([NFHS-5 via Down To Earth](https://www.downtoearth.org.in/economy/what-does-nfhs-5-data-tell-us-about-state-of-women-empowerment-in-india-80920)). Multi-profile is a requirement, not a v2 feature. Keep CareLink — it is built and it serves the remote case — but stop treating it as the answer to the caregiver question.

**Two things remain genuinely open, and should be marked as such rather than guessed.** First, there is no Indian survey measuring what proportion of elderly patients have a family member who fills the pillbox, orders refills or accompanies them to appointments, nor whether that person is typically a daughter, daughter-in-law, son or spouse. This is the single largest evidence gap in the entire research pass, and it is also the cheapest for this developer to close: a one-question prompt at first run ("Who are you setting this up for?") answers it from the real install base within weeks and is worth more than any literature search. Second, there is a documented Indian precedent for family members administering medication **covertly** when a patient refuses — recorded in psychiatric care, not general geriatrics ([PMC9707656](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9707656)). Any feature that lets a relative monitor doses has a real consent problem in this market, and the permission model should be visible to the patient on their own device, not silent.

## Eight in ten Indian users run a skin that kills your alarms

This is not an implementation detail. It determines whether the product does the one thing it exists to do, and it should be treated as a product constraint with the same weight as the user research.

Start with the market shape. Indian shipments are dominated by Chinese skins — **vivo around 18–20%, Oppo 14–16%, Xiaomi 13%, with Samsung the only non-Chinese volume player at 16–17%** ([Omdia via TelecomLead Q2 2026](https://telecomlead.com/smart-phone/top-smartphone-vendors-in-india-vivo-leads-as-omdia-reports-13-shipment-decline-in-q2-2026-126837); [Counterpoint](https://www.counterpointresearch.com/india-smartphone-share/) — the two houses disagree on the exact splits, so do not cite a precise share). On dontkillmyapp's aggressiveness league table, **Xiaomi, OnePlus and Samsung score the maximum 5💩, Oppo 4💩, and vivo, realme and Motorola 3💩**, while only AOSP/Pixel and Nokia score 0 ([dontkillmyapp.com](https://dontkillmyapp.com/)). Roughly eight in ten Indian users are therefore on a skin rated 3💩 or worse. Three specific behaviours matter most: Samsung puts **any app not opened in about three days to sleep, stopping background tasks including alarms**, and the setting may revert after an OS update or reboot ([dontkillmyapp.com/samsung](https://dontkillmyapp.com/samsung)); realme's **Sleep stand-by optimization** switches to low power when it believes the user is asleep, which is a direct threat to the night dose ([dontkillmyapp.com/realme](https://dontkillmyapp.com/realme)); and Xiaomi's autostart and background restrictions must each be flipped by hand through a different settings path ([dontkillmyapp.com/xiaomi](https://dontkillmyapp.com/xiaomi)). The Samsung rule is category-fatal if unhandled, because a medication reminder is precisely an app the user never opens.

Layer the platform rules on top. Roughly **68% of the Indian base sits on API 31+** ([Statcounter, India](https://gs.statcounter.com/android-version-market-share/mobile/india)), where exact alarms need a permission. On Android 14+ `SCHEDULE_EXACT_ALARM` is **not pre-granted to newly installed apps targeting 13+**, the affected-app criterion being literally that the app "isn't a calendar or alarm clock app," and restoring from backup does not restore the grant ([Android 14 changes](https://developer.android.com/about/versions/14/changes/schedule-exact-alarms)). Expo's own documentation states that **without an exact-alarm permission, scheduled notifications arrive at an approximate time, and `'alarmClock'` silently falls back to `'bestEffort'`** ([expo-notifications](https://docs.expo.dev/versions/latest/sdk/notifications/)). Silent is the operative word: there is no throw, no error, no signal.

**Against that, here is what MedTrack currently does.** `apps/mobile/app.json` declares `SCHEDULE_EXACT_ALARM`, which is the correct choice — `USE_EXACT_ALARM`, the variant granted automatically, is restricted by Google Play to apps whose core function is an alarm clock or calendar, and declaring it would block publication.

The problem is that nothing requests or checks it, and **in `expo-notifications@57.0.14` nothing can**. Verified against the installed package: there is no `getExactAlarmPermissionsAsync`, no `requestExactAlarmPermissionsAsync`, and no `delivery` option on any trigger type — not in `build/`, not in `src/`, and no such method is exposed from the Android native module. The decision is taken silently in Kotlin, in `ExpoSchedulingDelegate.kt:106`:

```kotlin
if (Build.VERSION.SDK_INT < Build.VERSION_CODES.S || alarmManager.canScheduleExactAlarms()) {
  AlarmManagerCompat.setExactAndAllowWhileIdle(...)   // exact
} else {
  AlarmManagerCompat.setAndAllowWhileIdle(...)        // inexact; Doze may defer by hours
}
```

No exception, no return value, no log. On Android 14+, where the permission is denied by default on a fresh install — roughly 59% of Indian handsets run Android 14, 15 or 16 ([Statcounter India, Aug 2026](https://gs.statcounter.com/android-version-market-share/mobile-tablet/india)) — every dose reminder is quietly approximate. Closing this requires `expo-intent-launcher` or a small config plugin; it cannot be done from the current JS surface.

There is a second, compounding defect: `MAX_SCHEDULED = 50`, chosen in a comment that cites **iOS's 64-notification cap** — a cap that does not apply on Android, where the practical ceiling is an undocumented and OEM-variable **~500 alarms per UID** ([Debugging Alarms with ADB](https://proandroiddev.com/debugging-alarms-with-adb-c8de7096b506)). For the modal Indian patient — **75.4% take three or more medicines daily** ([IJCDW 2026](https://ijcdw.org/survey-on-the-impact-of-pill-color-on-medication-adherence/)) — fifty notifications is roughly three days of horizon at five medicines and three daily doses.

The third defect is narrower than it first appears, and worse. `rescheduleReminders` is called from three places, not one: the React effect on `cache.medicines`, the Settings screen, and `sync/index.ts:76`. Because `actOnDose` ends in `void runSync()`, actioning a dose *does* top up the horizon — **when the device is online**. But that call sits inside the `try` block *after* `api.post('/sync')`, so an offline sync throws before reaching it and the horizon is never extended. An app whose stated advantage is working without signal therefore stops scheduling reminders precisely when it is offline, with only ~3.3 days of runway — the same timescale as Samsung's three-day app-sleep rule. The two defects compound.

The corrective package is well defined. Set the exact-alarm permission as a blocking onboarding gate with `canScheduleExactAlarms()` checked on every resume, deep-link to `Settings.ACTION_REQUEST_SCHEDULE_EXACT_ALARM`, and reschedule on `ACTION_SCHEDULE_EXACT_ALARM_PERMISSION_STATE_CHANGED` alongside boot and package replacement ([Android developers](https://developer.android.com/develop/background-work/services/alarms/schedule)). Platform-split the horizon so Android carries weeks rather than days, under a self-imposed ceiling. Top up from the notification response handler. Build an OEM wizard keyed on `Build.MANUFACTURER` with the per-vendor settings paths, and — because none of those settings is readable by an API — add a **silent heartbeat alarm plus a delivery log**, surfaced in-app as an honest "your reminders are / are not currently working" indicator. That heartbeat is the only way to detect OEM killing in the field, and it is also the only way this developer will ever learn the real failure rate, which nobody has published.

Two policy points worth locking in. MedTrack chose `SCHEDULE_EXACT_ALARM` over `USE_EXACT_ALARM`, and that is the right call: Play restricts the latter to apps whose **core functionality** is an alarm, timer or calendar, and apps that do not meet the criteria "will be disallowed from publishing" ([Play policy](https://support.google.com/googleplay/android-developer/answer/9888170)). And every health app, including those with no health features, must complete the **Health apps declaration** in Play Console and publish a non-geofenced, non-PDF privacy policy both in Console and inside the app ([Play Console Help](https://support.google.com/googleplay/android-developer/answer/14738291)).

Finally, the strategic implication. If reminder delivery on the dominant Indian phone cannot be guaranteed in code, then a reminder-only product is built on sand, and a second channel is not a nice-to-have. The one well-documented Indian health intervention here is instructive: rural mDiabetes used **automated voice calls twice weekly for six months** and saw belief that diabetes is preventable rise from 25.5% to 69.5% with over 90% recalling the messages ([PMC11818752](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11818752)), whereas the earlier SMS programme got **15.6% response from 31,725 registrants** ([ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/326503094_MDiabetes_initiative_using_text_messages_to_improve_lifestyle_and_health-seeking_behaviour_in_India)) and a two-country SMS RCT found **no significant reduction in progression to diabetes over two years** ([Diabetologia 2019](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00125-019-05061-y)). The two mDiabetes figures were measured differently and are not strictly comparable, but the direction is consistent and it points away from push notifications as the sole channel.

## DPDP binds in May 2027; CDSCO binds the moment the app advises

*This section is research, not legal advice. Every date, threshold and exemption below should be confirmed against the Gazette of India and with Indian counsel before it drives a shipping decision. Several points rest on law-firm summaries rather than primary text, and are flagged.*

### The dated DPDP verdict

The DPDP Rules, 2025 were notified on 13 November 2025 with deliberately staggered commencement ([AZB & Partners](https://www.azbpartners.com/bank/indias-digital-personal-data-protection-act-phased-rollout-and-key-compliance-milestones/)).

| Tranche | Date | What switches on | Relevance to MedTrack |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 13 Nov 2025 (live) | Rules 1, 2, 17–21: definitions and the Data Protection Board's constitution | None directly |
| 2 | ~13 Nov 2026 (≈3 weeks away) | Rule 4: Consent Manager registration; ss. 6(9), 27(1)(d) | None — no Consent Manager is registered and the Board has no members to register one |
| 3 | ~13 May 2027 | Rules 3, 5–16, 22, 23: notice, security safeguards, breach reporting, retention/erasure, children's data, SDF duties, cross-border, data-principal rights. IT Act s.43A repealed | **This is the date that binds the product** |

**Nothing in the DPDP Rules legally constrains this app's product behaviour today.** The Board exists on paper but a LiveLaw analysis dated 1 August 2026 records that it had **no appointed Chairperson and no appointed Members** ([LiveLaw](https://www.livelaw.in/articles/india-data-protection-board-established-law-543751)), so there is currently no body able to receive a breach report or impose a penalty. Two caveats carry real weight. First, MeitY proposed at a 22 January 2026 stakeholder meeting to **cut the compliance runway from 18 months to 12**, which would collapse the main deadline to around 13 November 2026 ([S.S. Rana & Co.](https://ssrana.in/articles/meity-plans-to-cut-short-dpdp-compliance-timeline-and-notify-cross-border-restrictions-for-sdfs/)); no Gazette amendment was found as of October 2026, and the Government reaffirmed the existing dates in a written Lok Sabha answer on 12 August 2026 ([indiadatalaw.org](https://indiadatalaw.org/deadlines/)). Build to the May 2027 spec, but do not schedule the work *for* May 2027. Second, the dates themselves vary by one to two days across law-firm summaries (AZB gives 12 November 2026 / 12 May 2027; Shardul Amarchand's annexure gives the 14ths) depending on the Gazette publication date — immaterial for planning, material if you are counting to a deadline.

A structural point that surprises people: **DPDP has no sensitive-personal-data category.** Health data is ordinary personal data with the same baseline obligations as an email address ([LiveLaw](https://www.livelaw.in/articles/cross-border-transfer-healthcare-data-reconciling-india-dpdpa-gdpr-542388)), unlike GDPR Article 9, which prohibits health processing unless a second condition is met ([Jentis explainer](https://www.jentis.com/blog/what-is-article-9-of-the-gdpr-sensitive-data-explained-and-how-companies-remain-compliant)). Health sensitivity re-enters only at the penalty-quantum stage, where the Board weighs the type of data affected. So treating medication data to an Article 9 standard is good practice and future-proofing, not a legal requirement. One honest gap: the IT Act's s.43A and the SPDI Rules 2011 are not repealed until the May 2027 tranche, and the SPDI regime does treat medical records as sensitive — but this research pass **did not source a citation for that treatment**, so the interim position should be checked rather than assumed.

What to build, and where MedTrack falls short. The notice under Rule 3 must be **standalone and unbundled** from terms of service, in plain language, with an **itemised** description of the data, the purposes, a description of the service, and live links to withdraw consent, exercise rights and complain to the Board ([Conventus Law](https://conventuslaw.com/featured-content/building-indias-digital-privacy-framework-an-overview-of-the-dpdp-rules-2025/)). Withdrawal must be **about as easy as giving consent** ([Taxmann](https://www.taxmann.com/post/blog/analysis-indias-dpdp-act-and-rules/)) — a literal UI requirement and the easiest one to fail. MedTrack's `ConsentRecord` model is a good instinct executed at the wrong granularity: its `ConsentKind` enum is document-level (`terms_of_service`, `privacy_policy`, `health_data_processing`, `caregiver_sharing`, `marketing_email`) where the Rules call for one record per data item × purpose. Storing the schedule to deliver reminders, storing adherence history, sharing with a caregiver, sharing with a clinician, crash reporting and any research use are **different purposes** and bundling them into `health_data_processing` is the classic "free, specific, unconditional" failure. Withdrawal must trigger real erasure under Rule 8, which means a tested erasure job, not MedTrack's existing `archivedAt` soft delete — while Rule 6 imposes a **one-year floor on logs**, so processing-event logs must live apart from the personal-data store. Breach reporting under Rule 7 has **no materiality threshold at all**: notify every affected person without delay and file a detailed report to the Board **within 72 hours** ([AMSS](https://www.amsshardul.com/insight/enforcement-of-the-dpdp-act-and-notification-of-the-dpdp-rules/)). Since failing to report (₹200 crore ceiling) is penalised almost as heavily as causing the breach (₹250 crore), the engineering rule is to report everything and never make a materiality judgement; the templates should be written now, because 72 hours is not enough time to draft them. A grievance channel must be published with a 90-day outer limit, and a contact point for processing questions must be prominent; a formal DPO is **SDF-only** ([Conventus Law](https://conventuslaw.com/featured-content/building-indias-digital-privacy-framework-an-overview-of-the-dpdp-rules-2025/)).

Two items deserve separate emphasis. **Language**: the data principal may access the notice in English or any of the **22 Eighth Schedule languages**, at the point of the request — an architecture decision today, not a translation task later. MedTrack has no internationalisation at all, so every notice and consent string needs externalising and versioning, with the language a user consented under recorded alongside the consent. Whether all 22 must be pre-translated or provided on request is unresolved in the sources. **Children**: a child is anyone **under 18**, verifiable parental consent is required, and the Fourth Schedule Part A exemption is **entity-based** — it reaches clinical establishments and healthcare professionals, not app publishers ([S.S. Rana](https://ssrana.in/articles/ensuring-child-safety-under-the-dpdpa/)). The cheapest compliant posture for a consumer medication app is 18+ enforced at signup, noting that a guardian-managed child profile still processes a child's data and still engages s.9. The penalty band here is ₹200 crore, so this is not cosmetic. And explicitly: **no SDF thresholds exist.** Designation is discretionary under s.10(1) and by notification only; any source quoting a user-count threshold for Significant Data Fiduciary status is wrong.

### The dated CDSCO verdict

CDSCO issued a **final Guidance Document on Medical Device Software on 21 July 2026**, Doc No. CDSCO/MD/GD/MDSW/01/2026, applying a **function-based, intended-purpose test** under the Medical Device Rules, 2017 ([Nishith Desai Associates](https://nishithdesai.com/research-and-articles/hotline/pharma-healthcare-update/from-code-to-compliance-decoding-indias-medical-device-software-guidance-15623)). CDSCO frames it as reflecting current practice rather than new regulation ([PureGlobal](https://www.pureglobal.com/news/india-cdsco-medical-device-software-guidance-2026)).

**Verdict: a pure reminder-and-log app is defensibly outside the definition, and the margin is thinner than it looks.** The exclusion that covers it is for software limited to data transfer, storage, archiving, formatting or communication; MedTrack stores user-entered data and issues time-based notifications, with no diagnosis, no physiological quantification, no real-time monitoring and no decision support. But "medical purpose" expressly extends to **monitoring, prevention, mitigation, prediction and alleviation** of disease, and the test is function-based — **one additional medical-purpose function can make the whole product a device**, even if its other functions are excluded. Carry this caveat honestly: the guidance **does not name medication reminder, adherence or dosage software as a category**, so the classification above is inference from the general test, not a cited CDSCO position. Reading the official PDF directly is the single highest-value legal follow-up available.

The claim discipline that keeps the app on the safe side is concrete. State an explicit non-medical intended use in-app, in the store listing and in the terms: an organiser and record-keeping tool for medicines the user has already been prescribed. **Never name a disease, disorder or pathological condition in a claim** — general-wellness software "cannot refer to diseases, disorders or pathological conditions" ([MedTech Spectrum](https://www.medtechspectrum.com/medicaldevice-softwareindia)). Avoid the regulated verbs — diagnose, treat, prevent, monitor, mitigate, predict, alleviate, cure, manage [a condition] — and avoid "clinically proven", "medical-grade" and "doctor-recommended" as efficacy claims. Ship no drug-interaction checking, no dose calculation, no abnormal-vitals flagging, no triage, and no adherence output framed as a clinical conclusion. A disclaimer is necessary but does not rescue a device-like function, because the test is functional. A passive, exportable adherence PDF the patient hands to their doctor is materially safer than a live clinician-facing alerting dashboard, which reads as real-time patient monitoring.

MedTrack is in good shape here with two specific exceptions, both found in the current code. `packages/shared/src/adherence.ts` emits an insight for night-time misses reading *"Consider moving this dose earlier if your prescriber agrees"* — an app-generated suggestion to change dosing timing. The hedge helps; under a function-based test, deleting the advice clause and keeping the observation is cheaper than defending it. The same function's `struggling_medicine` and `declining` insights report observations with their denominators, which is the right pattern — keep that discipline and never let an insight cross from "here is what your log shows" into "here is what you should do about your condition". Separately, the app's marketing and legal copy currently names **no disease anywhere**, which is exactly right and should be defended — note that this rules out the "diabetes companion" or "hypertension companion" positioning, which is also the only consumer price point with any evidence behind it. That tension is real and is addressed below.

Telemedicine, e-pharmacy and clinical-establishment rules do not reach this product as built. The Telemedicine Practice Guidelines 2020 bind registered medical practitioners, not software ([IBA](https://www.ibanet.org/article/15234243-b534-45f6-b303-4919cfe9a2dc)); e-pharmacy remains governed only by draft 2018 rules that were never notified ([Bhatt & Joshi Associates](https://bhattandjoshiassociates.com/regulation-of-online-pharmacies-and-telemedicine/)). **A "reorder this medicine" button is a separate regulatory project**, not a feature.

## Take ABDM's drug registry; leave the rest alone

| ABDM component | Verdict | Why |
|---|---|---|
| **National Drug Registry** | **Probe this week; build on it if the API is genuinely open** | 123,000+ branded medicines, 10,000+ generics, ~29,000 substances, SNOMED CT-coded, with government-stated open APIs ([Digital Health News](https://www.digitalhealthnews.com/india-launches-national-drug-registry-under-abdm-bringing-standardized-medicine-information-nationwide)). Solves the hardest data problem in an Indian medication tracker. Appears to sit outside the HIE-CM empanelment regime |
| **ABHA number as a profile field** | **Add now** | 14-digit format, locally checksum-validatable. Zero integration, zero compliance exposure — provided the app makes no claim to be fetching anything |
| **NRCeS FHIR `MedicationRequest` shape** | **Adopt the vocabulary now** | Costs nothing today, makes any future import cheap ([NRCeS IG](https://nrces.in/ndhm/fhir/r4/)) |
| **M1 ABHA login via an aggregator** | **Defer** | Viable route — Avni, a serious open-source health project, chose Eka Care over direct NHA empanelment ([avni-product #1912](https://github.com/avniproject/avni-product/issues/1912)). But nobody could confirm whether an aggregator removes the integrator's own empanelment and audit obligation. Ask the aggregator in writing before costing it |
| **M1+M3 HIU over HIE-CM** | **Aspiration only** | High fixed cost, near-zero payoff today |
| **UHI pharmacy discovery** | **Track, cannot build** | Pharmacy, lab and vaccination discovery are listed as upcoming phases with no announced dates ([PIB via VisionIAS](https://visionias.in/current-affairs/news-today/2026-06-30/society/unified-health-interface-uhi-launched-along-with-other-digital-health-initiatives)) |

The case against HIE-CM integration is arithmetic, not ideology. Against roughly **94 crore ABHA numbers** there are about **105 crore linked records** — approximately **1.1 records per account**, meaning most ABHA holders have zero or one ([MSG review, 10 July 2026, via Srishti IAS](https://srishtiias.com/ayushman-bharat-digital-mission-abdm-abha-health-id-2026/)). Only **2.72 lakh of 5.33 lakh registered facilities run ABDM-enabled software** (same source), and registry registration is not record publication. The one peer-reviewed field study, at three government tertiary hospitals in Agra actively pushing enrolment, found **129,007 ABHA registrations against 537,278 OPD visits — 23.96%** — with paper registration still preferred ([PMC13078079](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13078079/)). Even where records exist, the schema does not guarantee usable data: medicine codes in the NRCeS `MedicationRequest` profile are bound to the MedicineCodes value set as an **example binding, not a required one** ([NRCeS](https://www.nrces.in/ndhm/fhir/r4/StructureDefinition-MedicationRequest.html)), so bundles may carry free-text drug names and scanned PDFs in a `Binary` resource. In other words, an "import my prescription" feature built on HIE-CM would still need name-matching or OCR — which is most of the work of simply letting the user photograph the strip, at none of the cost.

And the cost is real. Production access requires functional testing by an **NHA-empanelled agency** (self-certification prohibited), a **CERT-In-empanelled WASA audit** producing a Vulnerability Closure Certificate and a Safe-to-Host certificate, and NHA committee review; **adding features after the audit may require re-auditing at extra cost** ([Nirmitee certification guide](https://nirmitee.io/blog/abdm-certification-process-sandbox-to-production-guide/)). For a solo developer shipping weekly, that last clause couples release velocity to a third party's calendar. The integration also requires a **publicly reachable HTTPS callback endpoint** with correct clock sync, because the gateway calls you ([Nirmitee integration guide](https://nirmitee.io/blog/step-by-step-guide-for-abdm-integration/)) — which MedTrack's Express API could host, but which rules out ever going client-only. M1 alone reportedly cannot exit the sandbox (NHA support is quoted as requiring any two milestones, a claim the source itself flags as unconfirmed), so the minimum is M1+M3 with full consent-artefact handling, Fidelius decryption and **purge of records and keys on consent revocation or expiry**. Nobody publishes a rupee figure for any of it, and no source could say whether NHA empanels unincorporated individual developers at all — a potential hard blocker.

The ABDM adoption counts themselves conflict and should be presented as such: **93.95 crore** from the July 2026 Mission Steering Group review via a secondary summary, **97.54 crore by September 2026** from an unverified SSRN working paper, **880+ million as of May 2026** in an academic commentary, and **90 crore+** in a May 2026 PIB release reported second-hand because pib.gov.in returns HTTP 403 to automated fetches. None of these is a first-party read. The honest statement is "roughly nine hundred million accounts, around one record each".

One rider. ABDM integration would pull in the **Health Data Management Policy's localisation clause**, which in its 2022 revised draft stated personal data shall not be stored beyond India's boundaries ([MediaNama, June 2022](https://www.medianama.com/2022/06/223-new-health-data-management-policy/)) — and DPDP s.16(2) expressly preserves stricter sectoral rules. The final in-force text of that clause could not be confirmed. Since DPDP itself imposes **no localisation duty on a non-SDF app** and **no country has been restricted** under s.16, hosting in an India region today is not required — but it costs nearly nothing now and a great deal after a live health dataset exists. Host primary personal data in an India region, keep residency configurable, and watch analytics and crash reporting: **a crash reporter that ships a stack trace containing a medicine name is an unplanned cross-border transfer of health data.**

### Bucket C — aspiration

A **non-app delivery channel** — an outbound voice call or a WhatsApp message that reaches the patient without them opening anything — is the most interesting unbuilt thing here, supported by mDiabetes's voice-vs-SMS contrast and by three independent Indian vendors converging on the claim that Indian elders are WhatsApp-native rather than app-native. That convergence comes entirely from vendor marketing ([famai.in](https://famai.in/blog/medicine-reminders-parents-without-apps/); [mydawai.in](https://mydawai.in/blog/pill-reminder-app-vs-alarm-vs-pillbox)) and is weak evidence individually; it is suggestive collectively and worth one experiment, not a rebuild. Also aspirational: **ABDM HIU integration**, revisited only when NHA or a credible third party publishes consent-request volumes or structured-prescription counts rather than ABHA-account counts; **UHI pharmacy discovery** when it ships; and **hand-drawn-style pictograms** for medicine forms and dosing times, which the HCI4D literature favours over photographs and conventional icons ([Medhi et al.](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/254004140_Mobile_interface_design_for_low-literacy_populations)) but which must be comprehension-tested with the actual user group before shipping, because locally developed pictograms outperform standardised ones and untested icons give a false sense of safety.

**Monetisation belongs in this bucket and nowhere else.** The only India-specific figure available — a **₹200/month ceiling** for urban health-app subscriptions, with **₹500–1,500/month** achievable for condition-specific apps — comes from a single growth-consultancy article that presents it as an industry estimate with no underlying survey ([productgrowth.in](https://productgrowth.in/insights/healthtech/healthtech-monetisation-india/)). That is not strong enough to price against. What can be said with more confidence is that the only evidenced consumer price shape in Indian healthcare is **Apollo Circle's ₹299 per year at roughly 25% attach across 40 million registered users** ([Salil Kallianpur, Substack](https://mypharmareviews.substack.com/p/subscription-healthcare-in-india), an analyst post, not a filing), that Medisafe's **two-medication free cap since January 2026** effectively excludes the Indian polypharmacy patient ([Bearable](https://bearable.app/the-best-medication-tracker-apps-of-2026/)), and that the higher chronic-condition willingness-to-pay is contingent on condition-specific positioning — which the CDSCO claim discipline above forecloses. **The two constraints are in direct tension and the research does not resolve them.** The defensible reading is that there is no good direct consumer monetisation path for a focused adherence tracker in India at launch, that the reason the category is unoccupied may be that it cannot be monetised rather than that nobody noticed, and that the right move is to win on trust and retention first and treat the pricing question as unanswered.

## What the evidence cannot support

Several claims circulate in this space that this research specifically **could not substantiate**, and a recommendation must not rest on any of them.

**Do not assert that AYUSH switching drives non-adherence.** No Indian quantitative evidence was found that patients commonly abandon allopathic chronic-disease medicines for AYUSH; the retrieved literature frames AYUSH as an *add-on* under supervised NPCDCS integration, where dose reduction was a programme outcome, not patient-initiated abandonment ([Journal of Ayurveda and Integrative Medicine 2021](https://doaj.org/article/fcb4b89c286044df9f1d8557b1f64755)). **Do not quote the WHO "50% adherence in developed countries" baseline**; it was not verified against the 2003 primary source in this pass. **Make no claim about ShareChat, Josh, Jan Aushadhi or Kisan app UX** — these were searched for and returned nothing of substance. **The hypothesised Indian app complaint list — ads, OTP friction, forced login, forced permissions, notification spam — is unverified**; no Reddit or review corpus was indexable, and what the actual App Store and Trustpilot text shows instead is overwhelmingly fulfilment and money (late delivery, failed cancellation, stuck refunds, unreachable support). Treat the hypothesis as something to test in user research. **No SDF user-count threshold exists**; designation is discretionary and by notification only.

Several figures **conflict between sources** and should be presented as conflicts rather than averaged.

| Conflict | Positions | Reading |
|---|---|---|
| Indian adherence rate | Pooled non-adherence **39.3%** in T2DM with individual studies spanning **11%–68%** ([PMC12914141](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12914141/)); WHO SAGE2 gives **51%** adherence across 2,840 NCD patients ([PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37900158)); an ARMS-scored metro sample gives **23.7% adherent** ([PMC10550356](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10550356/)); other samples report **67.1%** and **91.28%** | The spread is driven by instrument, not population. **No adherence tool is validated for the Indian setting** ([PMC11653035](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC11653035/)). Never quote "India's adherence rate"; attribute to a named study and scale. In-app, show trend over time, never a benchmarkable percentage |
| ABDM accounts | 93.95 crore (MSG Jul 2026) vs 97.54 crore (SSRN, unverified) vs 880M (May 2026) vs 90 crore+ (PIB May 2026) | All second-hand; PIB returns 403 to automated fetches. Say "roughly nine hundred million" |
| Regional polypharmacy | 65% North-east in one version of the same review vs 72% North India in another ([Frontiers](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fphar.2021.685518/full)) | Regional splits are unreliable. The pooled 49% in 60+ is usable; the regional breakdown is not |
| Medicines as a share of out-of-pocket spend | **~63%** ([NHSRC](https://nhsrcindia.org/sites/default/files/2025-08/Identification%20of%20Factors%20Contributing%20to%20OOPE%20on%20Medicines.pdf)) vs **~30%** ([JGU/PGIMER exit interviews, 5,252 patients](https://pure.jgu.edu.in/id/eprint/4726/)) | Different populations and definitions. Do not average; cite whichever matches your population or neither |
| Indian OEM share, Q2 2026 | Omdia: vivo 18%, Samsung 17%, Oppo 14% vs Counterpoint: vivo 20%, Oppo 16%, Samsung 16% | Use the shape (Chinese skins dominant, Samsung the one non-Chinese volume player), not a precise number |
| Hindi text expansion | +25%/−15% in one cheat sheet vs **contraction** of 10–15% in another | The sources disagree on direction. The only usable rule is length-dependent: short labels expand 100–200% ([edzlms](https://edzlms.com/indian-language-elearning-hindi-tamil-telugu/)). Never fix the width of a short label; pseudolocalise your own strings |

A further set of numbers are **flagged unreliable at source** and must not carry a confident recommendation. The 2026 diabetes meta-analysis self-rates its evidence **GRADE very low**, with **I² = 97.3%**, a prediction interval of **10.7%–76.5%**, Egger's p = 0.027 for publication bias, and at least three internal inconsistencies including an inverted confidence interval — use its per-study table, not its pooled estimate. The dontkillmyapp pages are visibly stale relative to 2026 software (the Xiaomi page stops at MIUI 14 with no HyperOS, the Oppo page is written against a 2016 handset, the vivo page does not mention OriginOS), so the settings paths must be re-verified on real devices. The `max_alarms_per_uid = 500` figure is one developer reading a device `dumpsys`, not an API guarantee. The claim that Android 16 requires `USE_FULL_SCREEN_INTENT` could not be confirmed in official documentation. The only rigorous strip-OCR benchmark found is **Indonesian and tested on synthetic degraded labels** ([MedSnap](https://github.com/AudiJRahardja/MedSnap)). The dosing-pictogram evidence is **Thai**, not Indian ([IJPS](https://www.ijpsonline.com/articles/development-and-evaluation-of-a-pictogram-for-thai-patients-with-low-literate-skills-3590.html)). The 76%/63% older-adult barrier rankings come from mostly non-Indian studies. The "two milestones to exit the ABDM sandbox" rule is flagged as unconfirmed with NHA by the source that states it. And the APK-size/conversion relationship is a **2017–2018 benchmark** that predates mandatory App Bundles.

Finally, the holes that no amount of further searching would have filled, and which bear directly on decisions here. There is **no Indian survey of who manages an elderly parent's medicines**. There is **no study of handwritten versus typed prescription prevalence in India**, and none measuring how patients identify their medicines beyond the single IJCDW survey — which did not measure shape or size, and whose sample is **0.9% South Indian**. There is **no evidence that Indian patients can successfully photograph their own strips**, and no OCR benchmark against real Indian pharmaceutical packaging; nothing at all was found on Indian blister-strip layout conventions. There is **no measurement of script-reading ability** among users who prefer Indic-language content, which is the single most decision-relevant missing number for the vernacular roadmap. There is **no measurement of Devanagari matra clipping on Android** — treat it as an empirical test to run, not a researched fact. There is **no field-measured alarm failure rate per OEM**. And there is **no evidence that app-based reminders improve adherence in an Indian population**: only **2 of 22** Indian adherence RCTs tested a technology-based intervention at all ([Frontiers in Public Health 2023](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/public-health/articles/10.3389/fpubh.2023.1194919/full)).

## Conclusion

The research reframes this product from a memory aid into an instrument of continuity of care, and that reframing is more consequential than any individual feature. A reminder-first app optimises for the failure mode that is easiest to describe and least likely to be decisive — every Indian digital adherence tool built so far has made the same bet, and the one that scaled nationally moved population outcomes by 0.2 points in the wrong direction. The features that address the measured failures are already half-built in MedTrack and are currently positioned as accessories: stock tracking, appointments, and a notion of why a medicine exists. Promoting those is a repositioning exercise, not a rewrite.

Two things follow that the literature cannot give and the install base can. The first is reliability: nobody has published a field failure rate for scheduled alarms on Indian Android skins, which means the heartbeat diagnostic in Bucket A is not just a bug fix — it is the only way this product will ever know whether it works, and the resulting data would be genuinely novel. The second is the primary-user question, which the two notes files could not settle because the literature measures who swallows the pill and never measures who operates the phone; a single first-run question answers it from real users in weeks. Both are instrumentation, both are cheap, and both convert the largest open questions in this research from unknowables into measurements. The uncomfortable corollary is that the category may be unoccupied for a reason: the strongest evidence here supports the clinical need and the design brief, and the weakest evidence by far is anything suggesting someone will pay for it. Build it small, build it honest about what it can and cannot do, keep the claim discipline clean enough that CDSCO never has a question — and treat the business model as the open problem it is, rather than the one the research already solved.
